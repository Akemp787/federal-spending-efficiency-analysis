Finding 01 · Growth

Nearly all of the 23% headline growth since FY2021 is the price level, not more buying

Nominal contract obligations rose from $633B in FY2021 to $778B in FY2025, an increase of 22.9%. Restated in constant FY2025 dollars using the GDP price deflator, the same series grows 4.3% — roughly $118B of the apparent increase is inflation rather than additional goods and services.

Finding 02 · Competition

Competition fell 4.0 points in FY2025, and 93% of that is one department

The share of obligations awarded competitively dropped from 70.3% to 66.3%, the largest single-year decline in the window. A shift-share decomposition separates two explanations that carry opposite implications: agencies competing less of their own work (within effect, -3.06 pp) versus dollars moving toward agencies that were already less competitive (mix effect, -0.76 pp).

The decline is behavioural, not compositional. The Department of Defense alone accounts for -3.67 pp of the -3.97 pp total, driven by its own competed share falling from 58.0% to 52.7% while its obligations grew.

Finding 03 · Attribution

Inside Defense, one component accounts for 76% of the move — and roughly 70% of the government-wide decline

Departments do not award contracts; their components do, under separate acquisition commands. Running the same decomposition one level down localises the Defense change almost entirely to the Department of the Navy, which contributed -3.98 pp of the department's -5.25 pp move.

Navy's own competed share fell from 45.8% to 36.6% on $176.6B of obligations — a nine-point drop against a five-year range of 39–46%. The Air Force moved the other way, which is what makes this a Navy question rather than a defence-wide one. Had Navy held its FY2024 rate, $16.2B more would have been competed.

Finding 04 · Control

Adjusting for what each agency buys, the mix explains 6.1 points of Defense's gap — and leaves 23.5

The standard objection to a low competition rate is that the mission requires sole-source buying. That is testable. Holding the product mix constant at the government-wide basket and letting only within-category rates vary — direct standardisation, the method used to compare mortality across populations of different ages — separates what an agency buys from how it buys it.

Defense's observed rate is 50.9%. Adjusted for its portfolio it is 57.0%, against a civilian median of 80.5%. Its product mix accounts for 6.1 percentage points; the remaining 23.5 points is competing less than other agencies do within the same product categories.

Product and Service Codes are coarse — "guided missiles" holds both a sole-source integration contract and a competitive components buy — so 23.5 points is an upper bound on the practice gap, not a point estimate. Finer strata would shrink it. Reference coverage is reported per agency so a thin-cell result is never mistaken for a robust one.

Finding 05 · Timing

The year-end surge reached a five-year high: 19.0% of the year's obligations landed in September

Annual appropriations expire on 30 September, and the observable result is a spike of obligations in the final weeks. In FY2025, September carried 19.0% of the year's contract dollars against an even-pace expectation of 8.3% — $83.4B more than a level pace would have placed in that month, and the highest concentration in the five years observed.

September concentration by fiscal year

September spending is not waste by itself — programme cycles genuinely land at year end. It is a risk indicator: awards made against an expiring appropriation are the population where schedule pressure is highest, which is why they are worth sampling first rather than accusing.

Finding 07 · Prioritisation

A composite index ranks where to look first — and the sensitivity test says which parts of that ranking to trust

The Contract Efficiency Index combines five dimensions — competition, contract-type risk, vendor diversity, year-end discipline and real-growth stability — into one score per agency. Because the weights are a judgement, every agency is re-scored under 2,000 random weightings drawn uniformly from the simplex. The bar is the configured score; the whisker is the 5th–95th percentile across those weightings.

Read the whiskers, not the ranks. Only 4 of 19 agencies hold the same quartile across the weighting distribution (Department of Agriculture, Department of the Treasury, Department of State, Agency for International Development). The middle of the table is not separable, and this index is a triage device for deciding where to look — not a verdict on how any agency performed.

Method & limits

What this analysis can and cannot support

  • Obligations, not outlays. Figures are net federal_action_obligation, so de-obligations reduce totals. An agency can post a negative fiscal year when terminations exceed new awards.
  • Contracts only. Award types A, B, C, D; grants, loans and direct payments are out of scope. IDV vehicles are excluded to avoid double-counting the orders placed against them.
  • Competition is measured on the current action. FPDS code E, follow-on to a competed action, counts as not competed; the sensitivity run re-scores under the opposite convention.
  • Contract type is not a verdict. Research and complex services legitimately require cost-reimbursement; the measure sizes the population carrying government cost risk, it does not allege misuse.
  • Vendor HHI is truncated at each agency's top 100 recipients and computed against true agency totals, making it a strict lower bound.
  • Nothing here identifies waste, fraud or abuse. Every output is a prioritisation signal that tells a reviewer where contract-level examination would be most productive.
